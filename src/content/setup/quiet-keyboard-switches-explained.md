> **Bottom line:** A cautious guide to switch feel, sound sources, construction, and the limits of labels such as silent, linear, tactile, and clicky. Check layout, switch feel, and return policy before buying; test existing boards first.

Keyboard sound does not come from a switch label alone. The key can make noise when pressed down, when it returns, through an intentional click mechanism, and through vibration in the keycap, plate, case, and desk. This guide is for readers who want to understand those sources without treating “silent” as a guarantee.

## Switch characteristics at a glance

| Switch type | Feel | Sound source | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear | Smooth, no bump | Bottom + return impact | Case still contributes |
| Tactile | Bump in travel | Bump + impact | Feel ≠ quiet guarantee |
| Clicky | Intentional click | Click mechanism + impact | Sharpest character |
| Silent | Damped bottom/return | Reduced impact, case remains | Label not standardized |

## Broad switch characteristics

A **linear** switch aims for a continuous press without a designed tactile bump. A **tactile** switch adds a change in resistance during travel. A **clicky** switch adds an intentional audible event. These words describe broad behavior, not a complete sound profile.

Some switches sold as **silent** add internal damping near the bottom or return of travel. Damping can reduce particular impact sounds, while the case and keycap still contribute. A quiet membrane or scissor keyboard can be less disruptive than a mechanical board, depending on construction and how it is used. Technology category alone does not decide the result.

## Criteria beyond the label

Consider the force required to start and continue a press, travel distance, key stability, keycap shape, case resonance, and whether the board shifts on the desk. Sound character can matter as much as volume: a sharp click, hollow case note, and soft low impact are different even without a trustworthy measurement.

A complete keyboard also brings layout, connection, firmware, repair, warranty, and return considerations. Replacing switches is practical only on a compatible board and may involve tools or warranty consequences. Do not assume a switch sold separately fits an existing keyboard.

## No-purchase checks

Move the keyboard away from a hollow surface, clear objects that rattle against it, and type with the force normally used rather than deliberately striking harder for a comparison. An existing laptop keyboard or spare board may already be quieter for a shared room. Software cannot change physical switch noise, but keyboard controls are optional in many browser tasks where touch or a pointer is available.

## What NoCharge evaluated

This is editorial research into switch categories and sound sources. NoCharge did not use sound meters, compare products in a controlled room, or hands-on test the Amazon results. We make no productivity, health, or comfort claim. “Quiet” remains contextual to the room, surface, user, and listener.

## Next action

Listen to the current board during ordinary use and identify the dominant event: intentional click, bottom impact, return impact, case resonance, or another object rattling. Solve that source first. If shopping remains justified, seek a returnable board with the required layout and treat merchant noise labels as a starting claim, not a measurement.
