> **Bottom line:** Use a repeatable four-by-four scan to turn each Memory Match reveal into useful location information.

A Memory Match board looks simple until a few mismatches leave symbols scattered across sixteen positions. The useful response is not to hunt randomly for a familiar picture. It is to give every reveal a location and to revisit the board in the same order. That makes the board easier to read even when the symbols are shuffled for every new game.

[Play Memory Match](/games/memory-match/) while you read, then keep the [definitive Memory Match guide](/guides/memory-match/) nearby for the exact rules and controls.

## Start with a fixed map

The NoCharge board is a four-by-four grid with eight symbol pairs. Before your first selection, number the spaces in your head from left to right and top to bottom:

1. top-left through top-right for positions one to four;
2. the next row for five to eight;
3. then nine to twelve; and
4. the bottom row for thirteen to sixteen.

You do not need to announce numbers out loud or remember all sixteen at once. The point is to stop every location from feeling equally vague. “Blue circle at row two, column three” is much more actionable than “I saw blue somewhere in the middle.” If numbers feel faster, use them. If a row-and-column description feels more natural, use that instead.

## Use the opening to collect information

The first few moves are often about gathering information rather than finishing pairs immediately. Select two cards you have not seen. When they do not match, retain both locations. On the next move, reveal a new card first whenever practical. If it matches one of the remembered cards, use the second selection to complete that pair. If it does not, you have added another useful location.

For example, suppose your first move reveals a yellow circle at row one, column one and a purple circle at row one, column two. They turn back over, but those positions are now known. On the next move, a yellow circle appears at row two, column four. Do not use the second selection on another unknown card. Match it with row one, column one. That turns one remembered detail into a completed pair and removes both locations from the board.

This approach does not guarantee a match on every move. It does make each miss serve a purpose.

## Sweep consistently after a mismatch

When you need fresh information, scan in one direction. A useful default is left to right across the top row, then continue downward. After a mismatch, resume at the first unexamined position in that pattern. Do not jump to a random corner just because a symbol feels familiar; random jumps make it harder to know which spaces have already been inspected.

A consistent sweep also prevents a common mistake: reopening a card whose location you have not actually retained. If you are unsure whether a spot has been seen, treat it as unknown and continue the scan. A deliberate new reveal is usually more valuable than a vague guess.

## Store pairs, not a gallery of symbols

Try to retain a small set of pair-ready facts rather than a complete visual catalog. A pair-ready fact has both pieces: “orange diamond at row three, column one and row four, column three.” As soon as you know both locations, take the match on your next available move. Clearing it makes the remaining board smaller and reduces the number of symbols competing for attention.

For single sightings, attach one quick cue to the position: “green, lower left” or “white, near center.” Avoid inventing an elaborate story for every card. The game moves quickly enough that short, consistent labels are easier to refresh.

## Check your second choice before committing

Your first card can change the decision tree. Once it is face up, pause for a beat: have you already seen its partner? If yes, select the remembered location. If no, use the second selection to gather a new position that fits your scan. This brief check is especially useful late in a game, when most cards have been seen and a known match may be available.

The move counter rises after the second card in each attempt, so a systematic second choice is where the board-reading method becomes visible in your result.

## Turn the map into a habit

A board scan is not a promise of a perfect run. The layout is random and early information can still be forgotten. Its benefit is that every turn has a clear job: complete a known pair or learn two new locations. Over several boards, that gives you a reliable way to compare your own move counts without relying on luck or speed.
