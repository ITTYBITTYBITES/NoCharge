Memory Match is playable with a keyboard because each hidden card is a button. The practical challenge is not memorizing a new control scheme; it is keeping the board map clear while focus moves from card to card. A consistent keyboard rhythm makes that much easier.

Start a round of [Memory Match](/games/memory-match/) and keep the [Memory Match guide](/guides/memory-match/) open for the full control reference.

## Learn the basic input loop

Use **Tab** to move focus forward through the available cards. Use **Shift + Tab** to move backward. Press **Enter** or **Space** to reveal the focused card. After a matched pair is found, those cards are disabled and no longer take part in the normal keyboard sequence. Focus moves to another available card so you can continue from the remaining board.

The page also includes a shared game toolbar above the board. Its Pause, sound, full-screen or immersive, and New game buttons are standard buttons in the page’s focus order. Use them when you need them, then Tab back into the card grid. You do not need sound to know whether a selection matched; the card states and move display provide the needed feedback.

## Give focused positions a row-and-column name

Keyboard play is a good reason to use a fixed map. The visual board is four cards wide. As focus advances, think of the cards as row one, positions one through four; then row two; and so on. When you reveal a card, attach the symbol to a short location label such as “orange, row two column one.”

This label is useful even if the focus ring moves quickly. You are recording the board position, not the exact number of Tab presses from the top of the page. If you need to return to a remembered card, you can scan through the remaining buttons while counting the row and column rather than relying on an imprecise visual impression.

## Separate navigation from selection

A common keyboard mistake is pressing Space as soon as the focus ring reaches a card. Instead, navigate first and select only when you know why that card is the right next choice.

For a new-information turn, Tab to the next unexamined card in your scan order, press Enter or Space, then decide what the second selection should be. For a known match, navigate directly to the remembered partner before activating it. This separation gives you time to check your map and avoids accidental reveals caused by rushing through focusable elements.

The move counter rises only after the second card in an attempt, so there is no penalty for a brief pause between the first reveal and the second decision.

## Use a repeatable sweep

A left-to-right, top-to-bottom sweep works particularly well with Tab. At the start of a board, use forward Tab to inspect fresh cards in order. When you reach the end of a row, continue naturally into the next row. If you need to revisit a previous location, Shift + Tab is often shorter than wrapping all the way around, but choose whichever direction leaves your location count clear.

Matched cards disappearing from the sequence changes the number of Tabs between remaining positions. That is why row-and-column thinking is more reliable than trying to memorize raw key counts. After a pair is removed, reorient using the visible grid and continue the same sweep.

## Pause without losing the board

If you need to step away, use the shared **Pause game** control. Pausing blocks card input while preserving revealed cards, matched pairs, and the move count. If two nonmatching cards are in their brief reveal period, their pending turn-over is held safely until play resumes. The page also pauses when it becomes hidden or when the privacy settings dialog opens; a manually paused game is not automatically resumed by those events.

When you return, take a second to restate the current known pairs before you make another selection. That is usually more effective than trying to rush back into the previous pattern.

## Practice with one clear goal

A keyboard run does not require a different strategy from a pointer run. It benefits from more deliberate navigation. Pick a single goal for the next board: maintain the top-left-to-bottom-right sweep, convert every remembered pair immediately, or keep concise row-and-column labels. Then check the completed move total against your own prior runs.

For more on that number, see [How move counting works in matching games](/articles/how-move-counting-works-in-matching-games/). For the broader board-reading plan, see [A systematic way to scan a Memory Match board](/articles/memory-match-systematic-board-scan/). Then [play Memory Match](/games/memory-match/) and use the control method that feels most comfortable.
